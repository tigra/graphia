Santiago walked into Day 1 as the fire chief—a man who'd spent thirty years learning that hesitation kills. He saw Dmitri's sympathy as vague shelter, Yuki's questions as deflection, and he said so. What he didn't see was that his own method—demanding specifics, refusing to offer any—looked exactly like someone controlling a narrative. Yuki caught it first, then Leila, then Dmitri, and they moved against him together. Santiago wrote privately that Yuki and Leila were coordinating, that they had to be Mafia protecting each other. He was half right: they were coordinating, but not because they were guilty. They were just watching the same man dodge the same question four different ways.

Amara and Kenji sat quietly through all of it. Amara watched Santiago draw heat and noted that Kenji "kept his distance perfectly"—let Leila and Yuki do the work while she stayed measured enough that voting with them looked inevitable. Kenji wrote that Leila was "the real threat now, the one who sees patterns where most people just see noise." He was right about that too. Five votes went to Santiago. Two against. He was gone, and he was innocent.

What Leila didn't know was that she'd just removed the one person most likely to keep her alive. She wrote that night that she'd let confidence override caution, that the second Mafia was "someone I haven't been watching closely enough, someone who's been quiet or blending in." She was thinking of Kenji. She should have been thinking of the fact that Amara had voted with her, that Amara had stayed so measured and unremarkable the entire time that Leila never once wrote her name down as suspicious. Then Leila died, and Amara wrote: "Leila's the real problem now—she's listening instead of performing, which means she'll eventually notice patterns that don't fit." Past tense. Already gone.
