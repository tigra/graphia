The game turned on Amara's clarity, though no one knew it at the time. On the first day, while everyone else was circling each other with careful language—Rajesh deflecting, Kenji asking questions instead of answering them—Amara simply pointed it out. She saw the pattern and said it aloud, fast and breathless the way she always did, before anyone else had finished thinking. That directness was the real tell, not the way anyone sounded. Rajesh died for his evasion, voted out by people who were tired of the dancing, but Kenji survived the day by staying perfectly measured, letting the chaos happen around him while he took notes. He never had to commit to anything.

What Kenji didn't account for was that Amara would keep pushing. She wrote it down in the dark—that she needed to watch who was genuinely confused and who was strategically muddying the water, that Rajesh and Kenji had both tried to pull her into their frame, which felt wrong. She was still thinking about it when they killed her that night. The Mafioso felt her pressure the way a predator feels prey moving closer, and Kenji—who had spent two years building a network in this town, who had learned to see people as variables in a system—understood that she would eventually see him. So he eliminated the one person whose clarity matched his own strategic vision, the one person who didn't need evidence to recognize evasion.

But Amara's death was the mistake. Dmitri had been loud and aggressive from the start, which made him easy to dismiss as noise, but he was listening too—really listening, the way the record shows. When Amara died, he understood what it meant: the Mafioso had panicked. The quiet one had felt threatened by the loudest voice in the room. On Day Two, Dmitri and Yuki and Soren all saw it at once. Kenji's measured calm, his practiced redirects, his refusal to ever commit to a name—it wasn't the sign of an innocent person thinking carefully. It was the signature of someone who had learned to hide in plain sight, and Amara had been close enough to name it before they silenced her.

The vote that ended it was almost inevitable by then. Kenji voted no, of course—he had no other move left—but everyone else voted yes. The Law-abiding Citizens had won because they finally stopped listening to tone and started watching for who wasn't willing to be seen, and because Amara's ghost was still in the room, pointing at the pattern even after she was gone.
